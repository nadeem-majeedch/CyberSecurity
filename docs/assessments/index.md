# Assessments

This section is the course's examination hall. It contains the questions, the integrated level
assessments, the capstone briefs, and — unlike most public courses — the answer keys.

Nothing here requires an instructor, a login, or a paid platform. You open a page, attempt the work,
then mark it against a published key or rubric. The only thing that can be private is your own honesty.

!!! note "What changed, and why"
    The earlier policy kept answer keys unpublished. That made sense for a cohort with a marker, and
    it made the course unusable alone: a self-study learner had no way to know whether an answer was
    right. Keys are now published in separate files, marked **Answer key**, so you can attempt first
    and check afterwards. The reasoning is in
    [How Assessment Works §7](../policies/assessment-format.md#7-answer-keys-and-publication-rules).

Lessons also end with open-ended knowledge checks. Those stay unkeyed: they are prompts to reason aloud, and
some have no single defensible answer. The keyed bank here is the check you can mark, and every item maps to the
same outcomes, so an inline question you cannot resolve is usually covered by the matching module key.

Each module quiz carries five items: three multiple-choice with one unambiguously correct option, one
short answer, and one scenario. Every module in this course has a single principal lesson plus its labs, so a
module quiz is also a lesson quiz — five items covering that lesson's stated outcomes. Recall is not the target;
most items ask you to choose between two defensible-sounding options and say why.

## How to use this section

The order matters more than the questions.

1. **Finish the module's lessons and at least one lab first.** A quiz taken before the lab measures
   reading comprehension, which is not what the course certifies.
2. **Attempt all five items on paper**, in full sentences, without the lesson open. Ten minutes.
3. **Mark against the key**, and mark each item as one of three states: correct, wrong, or
   *right for the wrong reason*. The third state is the one that costs people interviews.
4. **Read the explanation even when you were right.** If the explanation contains something you did
   not know, treat the item as not yet secure.
5. **Record the result** in your progress file, with the weak area in words — not just the score. The
   [Assessment Guide](../start-here/assessment-guide.md#4-tracking-your-progress) has a format.
6. **Retake at seven days.** Five items is a sample, not a measurement; the second pass is the
   measurement.

Target: **4 of 5, with no right-for-the-wrong-reason answers**, before moving to the next module.

### Using a key honestly

A published key changes what a quiz can prove. Two rules keep it useful:

- **Attempt before you look.** Once you have read an answer, you cannot un-read it, and the item
  stops testing anything.
- **Do not study the key instead of the lesson.** The keys are explanations of *why*, written to be
  read after a wrong answer. Memorising 200 answers prepares you for 200 answers.

If you are working in a supervised cohort, your instructor may set a variant of these items. The
published key then covers the practice set only.

## What the components test

| Component | Tests | Marked by | Weight in level result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Module quiz | Vocabulary precision and the ability to choose between plausible options | You, against the key | 15% |
| Lab | Applied skill, evidence, and safety discipline | You, against the four-criterion lab rubric | 45% |
| Level assessment | Judgement under a time constraint, plus justification of decisions | You, against the published criteria | 40% |
| Capstone | A complete piece of professional work, defended | Instructor or a rigorous self-review | Pass / merit / distinction |

The level assessments are not longer quizzes. Each one hands you a brief, an environment and a time
box, and asks for an artefact plus a written justification of the decisions behind it. The marking
criteria for the written part reward *rejected alternatives* and *stated limits*, because those are
what separate a professional answer from a correct one.

## Marking yourself without flattering yourself

Two habits do most of the work:

- **The replay test.** Hand your notes to someone who was not there — or reread them in a week — and
  reproduce the result. Every guess they have to make is a documentation gap.
- **The removal test.** Delete one step of your procedure. Can you say exactly what breaks? If not,
  you followed instructions rather than understanding them.

For the level assessments, mark the practical and written parts separately before adding them up. A
strong artefact with a vague justification is a "merit", not a "distinction", and marking it honestly
is what makes the next level's work easier.

## Bands

| Band | Score | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not yet | below 60 | The gaps are identifiable; revisit the named modules |
| Pass | 60–69 | You can do the task with reference material open |
| Merit | 70–84 | You can do it deliberately and explain why |
| Distinction | 85+ | You could defend it to a reviewer or a client |

No component may score below 50, and the safety criterion must reach "competent" — a submission that
violates scope fails regardless of its technical quality. Full rules:
[How Assessment Works](../policies/assessment-format.md).
