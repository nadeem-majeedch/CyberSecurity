# Assessment Guide

This page explains how you check your own understanding in a self-paced course, and how graded work is
judged if you are in an instructor-supervised cohort. The formal rules, rubrics, and integrity policy
are in [How Assessment Works](../policies/assessment-format.md); this guide is the practical version.

---

## Why self-assessment is the hard part

In a classroom, someone else tells you whether you understood. Alone, the temptation is to accept
"the command ran" as understanding. It is not: a command can run while you have no idea what it did or
when it would be dangerous.

The course therefore gives you three checkpoints per module, in increasing strictness:

| Checkpoint | What it tests | How it is marked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Knowledge check** | Recall and precise vocabulary | Written first, then checked against the published answer key |
| **Lab + rubric** | Applied skill and evidence | Self-marked against four published criteria |
| **Level assessment** | Judgement under realistic constraints | Self-marked (self-paced) or instructor-marked (cohort) |

---

## 1. Knowledge checks: the honest protocol

Every module ends with a handful of questions, and every level has a 200-item bank of them with
published answers under [Assessments](../assessments/index.md). Used carelessly, they teach nothing.
Use them like this:

1. **Close everything.** Write your answers by hand or in a fresh file, from memory, in full sentences.
2. **Only then** reopen the lesson and check each answer against what the lesson actually said.
3. **Mark three states, not two:** correct / wrong / *"I was right for the wrong reason."* The third
   category is where most hidden gaps live.
4. **For anything not fully correct**, write one line about what you misunderstood — not the right
   answer, the misunderstanding.
5. **Retake the check after 7 days.** Anything still shaky goes into your review list.

A useful threshold: **8 of 10 correct, with no wrong-for-the-wrong-reason answers**, before moving on.
Below that, re-read the relevant lesson sections; they are short by design.

Answers **are** published, on a separate key page per level, because a self-study learner has nobody
else to mark their work. The separation exists to protect one thing: the first attempt. Read the key
after you have written your answers, not while you are deciding what they are. A course that hands you
answers before you have thought trains recognition, not recall, and recognition collapses the moment a
lab goes sideways.

## 2. Labs: mark yourself before you feel good about it

Each lab carries a rubric with four criteria. Mark your own submission **before** you decide it is
finished, and be stricter than you would like to be:

| Criterion | The question to ask yourself |
| --- | --- |
| **Correctness** | Did I actually achieve the stated objective, or something adjacent to it? |
| **Methodology** | Could I explain why each step was necessary — and what would have happened without it? |
| **Evidence and documentation** | Could a competent stranger replay this from my notes alone? |
| **Safety and ethics** | Did I verify scope and isolation, and would I notice if I had drifted out of it? |

Two practical tests that catch most self-deception:

- **The replay test.** Hand your notes to someone else (or reread them in a week) and try to reproduce
  the result. Every place they have to guess is a documentation gap.
- **The removal test.** Delete one step from your procedure. Can you say precisely what breaks? If not,
  you followed instructions rather than understanding them.

A lab scoring 1 on safety is void regardless of everything else. That is not a formality: out-of-scope
testing is the one mistake that can end a career before it starts.

## 3. Level assessments

Each level ends with a practical task plus a written justification. The practical part is deliberately
open-book — professionals work with documentation open — and the written part asks you to defend your
decisions.

What distinguishes a strong level assessment:

- **Decisions are justified**, including the option you rejected and why.
- **Evidence supports every claim.** No "it appears that" without a capture, log line, or output.
- **Limits are stated.** What your evidence cannot show is part of a professional answer.
- **Remediation is specific.** "Patch the server" is not remediation; a version, a configuration, and a
  verification step is.

Level assessment domains and weights are listed in
[the assessment format](../policies/assessment-format.md#5-level-assessment-blueprint).

## 4. Tracking your progress

Because this site stores nothing about you, progress tracking is yours to keep. A simple system that
works:

```text
progress.md

## Level 1
- [x] CYB-101  done 2026-11-02  notes: yes  lab: yes  check: 9/10
- [x] CYB-102  done 2026-11-09  notes: yes  lab: yes  check: 8/10  (weak: risk vs threat)
- [ ] CYB-103  started 2026-11-12
```

Record the score and the *weak area*. The weak areas become your revision list, and the pattern across
modules tells you where to slow down.

## 5. Feedback and review

**Self-paced:** your feedback is your own notes plus the rubric. Two habits substitute reasonably well
for a marker:

- **Peer replay.** Swap lab notes with another learner and try to reproduce each other's work.
- **Explain it out loud.** Record a three-minute explanation of your findings. If you cannot fill three
  minutes without reading, you have not finished learning it.

**Cohort mode:** feedback within 7 working days for labs and 14 for level assessments, one resubmission
per lab within 14 days, and re-marking on request within 14 days of results. Details:
[feedback and moderation](../policies/assessment-format.md#9-feedback-resubmission-and-moderation).

## 6. Integrity, briefly

- Discuss concepts and debug together freely.
- Your evidence must come from your own runs.
- Cite everything, including documentation and blog posts.
- AI as a tutor: fine, with disclosure. AI-generated analysis passed off as your own: not fine.
- Never publish flags, model answers, or assessed walkthroughs.

The test: **can you defend every sentence and command in a live conversation?** Full policy:
[academic integrity](../policies/assessment-format.md#8-academic-integrity).

## 7. What the grades mean

| Band | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Below 60 | Not yet — the gaps are identifiable, and the module should be revisited |
| 60–69 | Pass — you can do the task with reference material |
| 70–84 | Merit — you can do it deliberately, and explain why |
| 85+ | Distinction — you could defend it to a reviewer or a client |

The bands describe capability, not effort. A learner who spends twice the time and reaches "pass" has
still passed; a learner who reaches "merit" by copying someone else's evidence has learned nothing that
will survive an interview.
